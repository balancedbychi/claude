# "I time travelled to Jack the Ripper's London in 1888! (Vlog)"

Format analysis of the `I time travelled to…` series + a full production-ready script.

---

## PART 1 — FORMAT ANALYSIS

### 1.1 The reference set

| Title | Runtime | Views | Age |
|---|---|---|---|
| I time travelled to the Titanic 1912! (Vlog) | 14:13 | 2.6M | 4 mo |
| I time travelled to Tudor London in 1536! (Vlog) | 12:27 | 2.3M | 3 mo |
| I time travelled to Ancient Rome! (Vlog) | 9:24 | 1.4M | 5 mo |
| I time travelled to Ancient Egypt in 2400BC! (Vlog) | 13:43 | 543K | 2 mo |
| I time travelled to D-Day in 1944 (WW2) | 15:00 | 339K | 1 mo |
| I time travelled to Edo Japan in 1657! (Vlog) | 13:52 | 296K | 3 wk |
| I time travelled to the Aztecs in 1520 #history | Short | 1M | — |
| Cleopatra gave me a palace tour #history | Short | 928K | — |

**Two patterns worth acting on:**

1. **Named-event topics beat general-era topics.** Titanic and Tudor London (a
   named ship, a named dynasty) cleared 2M+. "Ancient Egypt," "Edo Japan,"
   "Ancient Rome" — beautiful, but a browsing viewer has no specific question
   they want answered. Jack the Ripper is a **Titanic-class topic**: globally
   known, single event, built-in unanswered question. This should be a top-three
   video on the channel.
2. **Fear and disgust outperform wonder.** The two biggest thumbnails are a woman
   recoiling from raw meat and a woman terrified in front of a ship. The
   underperformers are open-mouthed *delight* (Rome, Egypt, Edo). The Ripper
   thumbnail must be **fear**, not wonder.

### 1.2 The title formula

```
I time travelled to [PLACE] in [YEAR]! (Vlog)
```

Fixed elements, all of which matter:
- **First person, past tense, British spelling** — "travelled," not "traveled."
  Consistency here is a brand signal; don't switch.
- **The exclamation mark.** Every long-form title has it except D-Day, where the
  subject made it inappropriate. That's a deliberate, correct instinct.
- **The `(Vlog)` tag.** Swapped for a bigger search keyword when one exists —
  D-Day used `(WW2)`. This is the slot for "Jack the Ripper."
- **Shorts drop `(Vlog)` and add `#history`**, and are often framed as a single
  incident rather than a place: *"Cleopatra gave me a palace tour."*

### 1.3 The thumbnail formula — five fixed elements

Every thumbnail in the set has all five. Miss one and it stops reading as the series.

1. **The selfie arm.** Visible in the bottom corner of the frame in nearly every
   thumbnail. This is the single most important element — it's what says *POV,
   first person, you are there* before anyone reads a word.
2. **Host center-frame, one big readable emotion**, matched to the era's promise.
3. **Period costume**, accurate enough to identify the era in a quarter-second.
4. **A crowded, chaotic background.** Never an empty landscape — faces, bodies,
   activity pressing in from the edges of frame.
5. **Giant text: PLACE + YEAR, in a typeface that codes the era.** Carved wood
   for Tudor, riveted black for Titanic, Trajan serif for Rome, brush for Edo,
   military stencil for D-Day, gold for Egypt. The font does era-setting work
   that the image doesn't have to.

### 1.4 Runtime and voice

- **Runtime:** 9:24 → 15:00, trending upward. Newer uploads sit at 13–15 min.
  Target **14 minutes** for the Ripper.
- **Voice:** first person, **present tense**, reacting in the moment. "Okay, so
  I've just arrived and —" The engine of the whole format is the gap between a
  modern person's expectations and historical reality. Comedy comes from that
  gap; so does horror.
- **The host is a guest, not a lecturer.** Facts arrive as things she notices,
  is told, or gets wrong — never as narration read over B-roll.

### 1.5 The vlog beat structure

The travel-vlog skeleton, applied to a century:

```
ARRIVAL       Disoriented cold open. First sensory shock — almost always SMELL.
THE STREETS   "Let me show you around." Establishing the world.
FOOD          Mandatory vlog beat. Eat something confronting on camera.
MONEY         What things cost, what people earn. Makes the era concrete.
A LOCAL       Meet someone. Dialogue, not narration.
THE TURN      The danger of this era. Tone drops.
THE EVENT     The thing in the title. Payoff.
DEPARTURE     Reflection. One idea to take away.
CTA
```

For the Ripper, "THE EVENT" is the autumn of 1888 and the manhunt around it.

### 1.6 The constraint that decides this video

This is a series about *murders*, made in a first-person format, for a broad
audience. The pivot that makes it work — and keeps it monetized:

> The host experiences the **fear and the aftermath**. She never witnesses anything.

Rules honored in the script below:
- No attack is shown, described, or recreated. No wounds, no weapons, no blood.
- The victims are named, given biography, and treated as people the host almost
  meets — not as a body count.
- The antagonist of the video is **poverty and the failure of the investigation**,
  not the killer. The killer is barely in it, which is historically honest.
- What the host is actually documenting is what 80,000 people lived through that
  autumn. That's the story, and it's a better one.

### 1.7 Production reality

These are AI-generated (image/video model, consistent character reference).
That means **character consistency is the production risk**, not the writing.
Lock a single reference sheet for the host in Victorian costume — same face,
same forearm tattoos, same shawl and bonnet — and reuse it in every shot prompt.
Every scene below carries a compact prompt block for that reason.

## PART 2 — PACKAGING

**Title — recommended:**
> `I time travelled to Jack the Ripper's London in 1888! (Vlog)`

**Alternate**, mirroring the D-Day keyword-in-parentheses pattern:
> `I time travelled to Whitechapel in 1888! (Jack the Ripper)`

**Thumbnail:**
- Host center-left, **fear** — eyes wide, glancing back over her shoulder, not
  smiling. Shawl over her head, dark Victorian dress.
- Selfie arm bottom-right, in frame.
- Behind her: a fog-choked gas-lit street, silhouetted figures, a newspaper boy
  mid-shout with a placard, a policeman's lamp in the distance.
- Text: **WHITECHAPEL 1888** in heavy Victorian condensed wood-type, dirty cream,
  top of frame.
- Grade: yellow-green sodium fog, deep shadow. Highest contrast of any thumbnail
  in the series — it should look *different* on the grid.

**Runtime:** ~14:00 · **VO word count:** ~2,000 · **Read speed:** ~150 wpm

## PART 3 — THE SCRIPT

`[TIME] SHOT — direction` · **HOST:** spoken to camera · `PROMPT:` generation note

### ARRIVAL

`[0:00] Selfie POV. Handheld, unsteady. Night. Fog. She turns the camera on herself, half-lit by a gas lamp, disoriented.`

`PROMPT: young woman, selfie POV, arm extended, Victorian working-class dress and wool shawl, fog-filled cobbled London alley at night, single gas lamp, yellow-green smog, 1888, cinematic, handheld`

**HOST:** Okay. Okay — I'm here. It's the thirty-first of August, 1888, and I am
standing in Whitechapel, in the East End of London.

`[0:12] She swings the camera around. Almost nothing is visible past ten feet. A figure passes and is gone.`

**HOST:** And I can't see anything. Like, genuinely — that's not the fog, that's
*coal smoke*. Every single house here burns coal, and there are millions of them,
and it just… sits. They call it a London Particular. It gets into your teeth.

`[0:32] Camera close on her face. She covers her nose.`

**HOST:** And the smell. I was warned about the smell and I did not take it
seriously. It's horse — there are three hundred thousand horses in this city and
they produce about a thousand tonnes of manure a day — and it's the drains, and
it's the slaughterhouses, because there are working slaughterhouses *in the
neighbourhood*, and it's the tanneries, and underneath all of it there's this
sweet rotting thing I can't identify and I've decided not to investigate.

`[0:58] She looks up at a street sign.`

**HOST:** Right. So. I've come here on a specific date for a specific reason, and
I'll be honest with you — I'm not sure I should have. Because in about four hours,
something is going to happen two streets away from where I'm standing that the
world still hasn't stopped talking about.

`[1:12] Beat. She looks off-camera into the fog.`

**HOST:** Let me show you where I am first.

`TITLE CARD: WHITECHAPEL 1888`

---

### THE STREETS

`[1:20] Daylight. The fog has lifted to a haze. A crowded market street — barrows, shouting, mud, children, laundry lines overhead.`

`PROMPT: crowded Victorian East End London market street, 1888, costermonger barrows, ragged children, overhead laundry lines, mud, documentary realism, daylight haze`

**HOST:** So this is Whitechapel in daylight, and the first thing you need to
understand is the *density*. Eighty thousand people live in this district. You
could walk across the whole thing in about twenty minutes.

`[1:38] She squeezes past bodies. The camera gets jostled.`

**HOST:** Everyone told me Victorian London was top hats and carriages. Two miles
west, sure. Here? This is one of the poorest square miles in Europe.

`[1:50] She ducks into a side alley. It goes instantly dark and narrow.`

**HOST:** And look at this — this is the thing. The main roads have gas lamps. But
the courts and the alleys behind them, where thousands of people actually live —
nothing. No lighting at all. A police constable walks past the end of a court like
this maybe once every twenty, thirty minutes.

`[2:10] She stands in the dark alley, looks into camera.`

**HOST:** Twenty minutes of nobody watching. Remember that. It's going to matter.

---

### THE DOSS HOUSE

`[2:20] Interior. A common lodging house — rows of beds, low ceiling, a fire, thirty people in one room. She's speaking quietly.`

`PROMPT: Victorian common lodging house interior 1888, rows of narrow iron beds, low smoky ceiling, crowded with poor men and women, firelight, oil lamp, gritty documentary`

**HOST:** Okay so I need to show you where a huge number of people here sleep,
because it explains almost everything about this story.

`[2:32] Wide shot of the room.`

**HOST:** This is a common lodging house. A doss house. There are two hundred and
thirty-three of them in Whitechapel alone, holding something like eight and a half
thousand people a night.

`[2:45] She holds up coins to camera.`

**HOST:** Fourpence gets you a single bed. Eightpence gets you a double. And if
you've only got tuppence you get the "sit-up" — which is a bench, in a room, for
the night. There's a story that some places rig a rope across the room and you
sleep leaning on it, and they cut it down at dawn. I've heard it from three people
and I've seen zero ropes, so — file that under maybe.

`[3:10] Close on her face, serious now.`

**HOST:** But here's the part that got me. You pay *before* you sleep. Every
night. No fourpence, no bed. And the deputy on the door will turn you out at
midnight without blinking, and then you are on the street until morning.

`[3:28] She looks around the room at the people in it.`

**HOST:** Thousands of people here are four pennies away from spending the night
outdoors. Tonight. And they're mostly women.

---

### FOOD

`[3:40] Street food stall. Steam. She's holding something in newspaper, examining it with visible reluctance.`

`PROMPT: Victorian London street food stall 1888, jellied eels and pie stall, steam, gas lamp, young woman in shawl holding food wrapped in newspaper, selfie POV`

**HOST:** Right, it's a vlog, so — obviously — we're eating.

`[3:48] She holds up the food.`

**HOST:** Jellied eels. Eels boiled in stock, and the stock sets into a jelly.
Cold. It is a *cold* fish jelly. This is genuinely one of the cheapest things you
can eat in London.

`[4:00] She eats. Long pause. Her face works through several stages.`

**HOST:** …it's the texture. It's not the taste, the taste is fine, it's savoury —
it's that it's cold and it *moves*.

`[4:12] She hands it off-camera to a delighted child. Cut to her buying something else.`

**HOST:** Okay. Second attempt. A ha'penny bloater — smoked herring — and a cup of
tea that I'm told is mostly not tea.

`[4:24] Close on the tea.`

**HOST:** And that's the actual food story here, which isn't funny at all. Food
gets watered down and bulked out at every step. Chalk and water in the milk. Alum
in the bread to make cheap flour look white. Used tea leaves, dried out, dyed, and
sold again. There are laws against it. Nobody's really enforcing them down here.

`[4:45] She sets the cup down.`

**HOST:** You can be a person who eats every day and is still starving. That's most
of this street.

---

### MONEY

`[5:00] She's sitting on a step doing sums on her fingers to camera.`

**HOST:** Let me do the maths for you, because it's brutal and it's quick.

`[5:08] Simple on-screen text stamps appear as she talks — but she reads them, she doesn't have them narrated over her.`

**HOST:** A bed is fourpence. Now — a woman doing casual work here, sewing, or
sacking, or laundry, might make eight or nine shillings a *week*, and that's if the
work turns up, which it doesn't reliably. A docker down the road is hired by the
day, every day, standing in a crowd hoping to get picked.

`[5:30] She gestures down the street.`

**HOST:** And this is 1888, so — just up the road at the match factory, the women
and girls making matches went on strike last month. Fourteen hours a day, fined
for talking, fined for dropping matches, and the phosphorus they work with rots
your jawbone. They struck, and they *won*, and it was a genuinely huge deal.

`[5:52] Back to camera, quieter.`

**HOST:** That's the world. Everyone here is one bad week from the street. Hold on
to that, because in about six hours the newspapers are going to start describing
the women who die here as if they came from nowhere.

---

### THE FIRST MORNING

`[6:15] Early morning. Grey light. The street is oddly still. People clustered in doorways, talking. She's filming discreetly, low.`

`PROMPT: Victorian London street dawn 1888, small crowds of working people gathered talking anxiously in doorways, grey light, police constable in distance, documentary`

**HOST:** So. It's the morning after, and — something's happened.

`[6:26] She keeps her voice down.`

**HOST:** A woman was killed a few streets from here overnight. In Buck's Row. Her
name was Mary Ann Nichols. She was forty-three.

`[6:38] Slow, respectful. Camera steady on her face — no cutaway.`

**HOST:** And I found out something about last night that I can't stop thinking
about. She was in a lodging house on Thrawl Street a few hours before, and she
didn't have her fourpence, and they turned her out. And the deputy said she'd have
to go, and she laughed and said she'd soon get her doss money — *look what a jolly
bonnet I've got now.* That's the last thing anyone recorded her saying.

`[7:04] She looks off down the street.`

**HOST:** She was out here because she was four pennies short of a bed.

`[7:12] She turns back to camera.`

**HOST:** I'm not going anywhere near where it happened. That's not what this is.
But I want to stay, because what happens to this neighbourhood over the next ten
weeks is honestly the part nobody tells you about.

---

### THE PRESS

`[7:30] Cut to energy — newsboys shouting, placards, a crowd grabbing papers. Fast handheld.`

`PROMPT: Victorian newsboys shouting with newspaper placards, crowd grabbing papers, London street 1888, motion blur, energetic documentary`

**HOST:** And the press has arrived. Oh, the press has *arrived*.

`[7:40] She holds up a paper to camera.`

**HOST:** So there's a newspaper called The Star that launched literally this
January — halfpenny, sensational, aimed right at working people — and this story
is about to roughly double what they sell. There are multiple editions a day.
People are buying *maps of Whitechapel* to follow along.

`[8:00] She flicks through pages, wide-eyed.`

**HOST:** And I need to tell you where the name comes from, because it is not what
you think.

`[8:08] Close on a letter reproduced in print, red ink.`

**HOST:** In a few weeks a news agency is going to get a letter, written in red
ink, taunting the police — signed with a nickname nobody had used before. "Yours
truly, Jack the Ripper." They print it. And it goes around the *world* in about a
week.

`[8:28] Back to her, genuinely amazed.`

**HOST:** Hundreds more letters follow. Police collect over five hundred. Almost
every single one is a hoax — and a lot of people now think that first one was
written by a *journalist*, to keep the story selling.

**HOST:** The most famous name in criminal history might be a marketing decision.

---

### THE FEAR

`[8:45] Night. Streets much emptier than before. Men in groups carrying sticks and lamps. Tone drops hard.`

`PROMPT: Victorian London night street 1888, groups of working men patrolling with lanterns and clubs, empty fog-filled street, tense, cinematic low light`

**HOST:** Okay. This is Whitechapel now, at night, a few weeks in. And I want you
to look at how different this is from the street I showed you at the start.

`[8:58] She films the men passing.`

**HOST:** Those are locals. A group of tradesmen formed a Vigilance Committee —
they patrol at night, they've put up their own reward money, they carry whistles.
The police won't offer a reward, so shopkeepers did it themselves.

`[9:14] She's walking. Glances behind her repeatedly. Genuinely uneasy.`

**HOST:** And I'll be honest with you, I'm a visitor here and I know how this ends
and I still don't like walking this street.

`[9:26] Cut: a wall, chalk writing, dawn light. Her voice drops.`

**HOST:** And there's an uglier thing happening. Everyone's decided the killer must
be a foreigner. There's been a huge amount of Jewish immigration into the East End
in the last few years — people fleeing violence in the Russian Empire — and the
suspicion lands on them immediately.

`[9:46] She indicates the chalk writing without reading it aloud.`

**HOST:** A message got chalked on a wall in Goulston Street blaming the Jewish
community. The Police Commissioner ordered it washed off before sunrise — before
anyone photographed it — because he was terrified that if the market crowds read
it there'd be riots.

`[10:04] She turns to camera.`

**HOST:** So the single best physical clue in the whole case survives as three
officers' handwritten copies. Which don't match each other.

---

### THE POLICE

`[10:15] A constable at a corner, lamp on his belt. She approaches. Reverse angle, dialogue implied.`

`PROMPT: Victorian Metropolitan Police constable 1888, custodian helmet, bullseye lantern, foggy street corner, night, talking to young woman, cinematic`

**HOST:** I've just spent ten minutes talking to a constable and I feel awful for
them, genuinely.

`[10:26] Walking, to camera.`

**HOST:** So here's what the police have. They're going house to house, thousands
of homes, thousands of lodging house beds. They'll interview over two thousand
people. Look hard at about three hundred. Detain about eighty.

`[10:42] She counts off on her fingers.`

**HOST:** And here's what they don't have. Fingerprints — Scotland Yard doesn't
start using those until 1901, thirteen years too late. Blood typing, no. DNA,
obviously no, that's the better part of a century away. Crime scene photography as
standard, barely. A national criminal database, doesn't exist.

`[11:02] Beat.`

**HOST:** They tried *bloodhounds*. They ran trials in a park and quietly dropped it.

`[11:08] She stops.`

**HOST:** And there's a structural thing nobody mentions. On one night in
September, two women are killed within an hour of each other — and one is in
Metropolitan Police territory and the other is half a mile away in *City of London*
Police territory. Two different forces. Two separate case files. Nobody fully
combines them for weeks.

`[11:30] To camera.`

**HOST:** People say this case was unsolvable. I don't think that's quite right. I
think it was unsolvable *in 1888*. He didn't beat the police. He beat the year.

---

### THE FIVE

`[11:45] Interior, the lodging house again, quieter now. Women by the fire. She sits among them. Warm light. This is the emotional centre — hold the shots longer here.`

`PROMPT: Victorian common lodging house kitchen 1888, group of working-class women sitting by a fire talking, warm firelight, intimate documentary portrait`

**HOST:** I want to end in here, with these women, because I think this is the part
that actually matters.

`[11:58] Slow, unhurried.`

**HOST:** Over ten weeks, five women are killed. Mary Ann Nichols. Annie Chapman.
Elizabeth Stride. Catherine Eddowes. Mary Jane Kelly.

`[12:12] Portraits held on screen longer than a normal cut. Music almost drops out.`

**HOST:** And for a hundred years they were basically a list. A category. Recent
research went back into their actual lives — their jobs, their marriages, their
kids, where they slept — and found that several of them were most likely attacked
while they were *asleep*. Sleeping outdoors. Because they had nowhere else to be
that night.

`[12:36] She looks around the room.`

**HOST:** Every woman in this room tonight paid fourpence. That's the entire
difference. That's it.

---

### DEPARTURE

`[12:50] Back out on the foggy street, alone. The opening shot, mirrored.`

**HOST:** So I'm going to go. And I think I expected this to be a story about a
monster, and it just — isn't.

`[13:02] Walking slowly.`

**HOST:** He was almost certainly not a genius, or a doctor, or a nobleman, or a
mastermind. He was very probably an unremarkable local man standing in a dark
alley in a city that hadn't invented the tools to catch him yet.

`[13:18] She stops under the gas lamp from the first shot.`

**HOST:** The reason this happened here isn't mysterious at all. Eighty thousand
people. No lighting in the courts. A patrol every half hour. And thousands of
women whose safety, on any given night, came down to whether they had four
pennies.

`[13:38] To camera, last line.`

**HOST:** That's not a mystery. That's just what it cost to be poor here. And
nobody's ever put that on a poster.

`[13:48] She turns and walks into the fog. Camera lowers. Cut to black.`

---

### CTA

`[13:52] End card.`

**HOST:** If you want me to go somewhere specific next, put it in the comments —
I've had about four hundred of you asking for Pompeii and I'm running out of
excuses. Subscribe, and I'll see you in another century.

`[END]`

## PART 4 — PRODUCTION NOTES

### Retention map

| Time | Risk | Device |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | The make-or-break drop | Smell. Visceral, immediate, unexpected — nobody opens with manure tonnage. |
| 0:58 | Post-hook sag | The threat is planted: "in four hours, two streets away." |
| 3:40 | Mid-video sag | Food beat — the eels. Pattern break, pure entertainment. |
| 6:15 | Tonal transition risk | Silence and stillness before the reveal. Don't rush this cut. |
| 7:30 | Post-gut-punch drop | Hard energy flip to the newsboys. The letter reveal is the second hook. |
| 11:45 | Pre-ending drift | Warm light, slower cuts, the payoff idea. |

### Safety / monetization pass

- No attack shown, described, or recreated. No wounds, weapons, or blood anywhere
  in the script or the prompts.
- The host is never in proximity to an incident. She explicitly declines to go.
- Victims named, aged, quoted, given circumstance. No body count framing.
- The Goulston Street message is referenced but **not read aloud** — it's an
  antisemitic line, and quoting it in a first-person vlog serves nothing.
- Antisemitism in the investigation is included as historical fact and clearly
  framed as a failure, not a theory.

### Character consistency (the real production risk)

Lock one reference sheet before generating anything: same face, same forearm
tattoos as the rest of the series, dark wool dress, cream shawl, hair pinned. Reuse
it as the character reference in every prompt. Two grades only:

- **Night:** yellow-green sodium fog, deep crushed shadow, lamp bloom.
- **Day:** desaturated grey-brown haze, no blue sky, soot on everything.

Never let a shot come back clean or pretty. If a generation looks like a costume
drama, regenerate — this series' credibility is in the grime.

### Accuracy notes

Solid: the Nichols "jolly bonnet" quote and the fourpence turn-away; 233 lodging
houses / ~8,500 beds in Whitechapel; the 1888 Matchgirls' Strike and phossy jaw;
The Star's launch in Jan 1888 and its circulation jump; the "Dear Boss" letter and
its widely-suspected journalistic origin; 500+ hoax letters; 2,000 interviewed /
300 investigated / 80 detained; fingerprinting adopted 1901; the two-force
jurisdiction split on 30 September; the Goulston Street erasure; the bloodhound
trials; the Vigilance Committee and its privately-funded reward.

Hedge, as the script does: the **two-penny hangover rope** is widely repeated but
poorly evidenced — the script explicitly flags it as unverified, which is also a
nice character beat. Keep it that way.

Do not claim the case is solved. The shawl-DNA/Kosminski claim is contested on
three counts (mitochondrial DNA can exclude a suspect but never identify one; the
chain of custody is broken; a century of contamination). It's deliberately left
out of this script — it's a *different video*, and a better one for having room.

### Derivative cuts

1. Short: "Fourpence was the difference between a bed and the street." — strongest
   single idea in the video.
2. Short: "I ate jellied eels in 1888" — pure format, high replay.
3. Short: "The name 'Jack the Ripper' was probably invented by a journalist."
4. Short: "Police washed the only clue off a wall before sunrise."
5. Long-form follow-up: `I time travelled to Victorian London's doss houses! (Vlog)`
6. Long-form follow-up: the DNA video — *"They say they've solved Jack the Ripper.
   I went to check."* Modern-day framing, breaks format deliberately.
