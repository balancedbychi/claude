01

The two-hander

One talking head gives advice. Two characters give a relationship the viewer can stand inside. Nia is the person in it. Chi is the person on solid ground. The viewer is Nia — and Chi is the friend she wishes was on the phone at 11:40pm.

That gap is the product. Let It Go exists for the moment between the urge and the action, and this format dramatises that moment in every single post. Critically: Chi never judges Nia, and Nia never gets fixed. She keeps struggling across the series. That's the brand promise made visual — nobody gets shamed for staying, returning, or finding it hard.

Her voice doesn't exist as an asset

The Element is created. The voice is a different problem, and worse than a missing file. All four Titanic generations passed a single image and nothing else — no audio input, no voice element, no voice parameter. Seedance 2.0 synthesised that voice on the fly from one line of prompt text: "warm low British-accented young female voice, dry and warm."

So the voice you want exists nowhere except baked into four MP4 audio tracks. Re-running the prompt gives a similar voice, not the same one — which is exactly the drift that kills a character posting daily. To keep it, clone it back out of the dawn recap clip (calmest ambient bed, driest delivery), then move Nia onto the same audio-first pipeline Chi already uses. Full procedure in the production notes.

02

Instagram is not TikTok

Your brief was written for TikTok's twice-daily hook-and-churn rhythm. It doesn't transfer cleanly. The consequential difference: Instagram reach is driven by sends per reach — DM shares to one specific person — not watch time alone.

So write for the send, not the scroll. A woman watching a Nia video doesn't think "that was good." She thinks "this is literally me and Danielle" and forwards it. Every two-hander is built so there's a moment where the viewer sees her own group chat.

The test before anything ships: who does she send this to, and what does she type with it? If you can't answer, it's a weak post.

One keyword per pillar so the automation routes cleanly and you can read intent from the keyword alone. 04b

Carousel anatomy

Slide one is a cover, not a slide — it's the only thing most people see, so it carries the hook and nothing else.

SlideDoes
1The hook. One line. Character portrait or a strong object shot.
2Name the problem in her words. Often flat colour, no image.
3–7One idea per slide. Alternate image and flat-colour slides so the swipe has rhythm.
8The turn — the line worth screenshotting. Usually Chi.
9The question, and the keyword prompt.
The background flips when the speaker flips. Nia slides on camel and cream, Chi slides on navy — so in a two-hander carousel the call-and-response is visible before a word of it is read. Nothing within 80px of an edge; Instagram crops the preview.

08

Guardrails

This audience is vulnerable and the offer makes claims easy to overstate. Check every script against this before it ships.

Never

The 100 replies and the 150-for-$17 pack in the same frame

The count itself is confirmed and safe to state. The framing is the live risk: 150 for $17 is better per-reply value, so side by side the entry offer reads as the bad deal.

Instead

"$17 to get in, 100 replies to start." Sell the way in; sell packs later, to someone who has used their hundred.

Never

"Unlimited" · "as much as you need"

The initial payment doesn't cover all future replies.

Instead

"Reply packs are there if you want to keep going — 50 for $7, 150 for $17. Use them at your own pace."

Never

"Like therapy" · "your therapist in your pocket" · any implied human coach

It is neither.

Instead

"An AI chat that talks it through with you." · "Somewhere to think it through."

Never

"You'll finally get clarity" · "this will heal you" · "he'll come back if you…"

No outcome promises, and never a relationship-outcome promise.

Instead

"A place to sort out what you actually want to do."

Never

"Join" · "enroll" · "doors open" · "spots" — or inventing calls, courses, coaching or community features

The membership isn't named, priced, dated or built.

Instead

"What's one standard you're ready to stop talking yourself out of?" — invite conversation, gather in the broadcast channel.

Never

Clinical language — attachment style, trauma response, dysregulated, narcissist, love-bombing, avoidant

Also: no claims about what he's thinking, no shaming anyone for staying or going back, no "should."

Instead

Plain words at roughly a sixth-grade level. Chi gives options, not orders.

The line that actually protects the brand. If a script drifts toward "don't let him treat you like that, block him, you're too good for him" — it's off-brand. That's the revenge version of this genre and it's everywhere already. The Standard Society is the version where you still love him and choose yourself anyway. That's the harder, rarer, more useful thing, and it's the entire differentiator.
Comment section. This audience will post real pain in your comments. Before week 1: decide who reads them and how often, have a warm holding reply ready, and know your line for "this needs more support than a comment reply" — with a real signpost ready, not a product link. Never reply to distress with a purchase prompt.
