# Higgsfield Creator Course Roadmap

Expanded foundation, specialist courses, and monthly content plan for a beginner Skool membership.

Updated September 23, 2026. This revision replaces the earlier six-course plan. It reflects the decision to have the complete core foundation ready at launch, with specialist courses released afterward. This is a curriculum and production plan; the courses, reference packs, and templates listed here still need to be produced and tested.

**Member outcome:** Choose a creative direction, build a consistent character and reusable scene assets, produce a finished piece of content, then develop one chosen content or business path.

**Teaching principle:** One direction, one character or format, one small project at a time. Members can explore the full library while following a focused route.

## 1. Organize the membership into four areas

| Area | Purpose | Availability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Foundation | Direction, visual language, tools, characters, sets, assets, and a finished first project | Complete at launch |
| Choose Your Track | Dance, transitions, UGC, faceless content, stories, and income paths | Staged release after launch |
| Creator Reference Library | Reusable worksheets, approved examples, prompts, asset records, and troubleshooting guides | Essential collection at launch; expanded with each course |
| Monthly AI Update | Tested changes, relevant tools, lesson corrections, and a practical recommendation | Monthly from launch |

The group can eventually cover the full range of requested topics without asking every member to study everything. Label courses Foundation, Ready Now, Planned, or Under Revision. Keep release dates provisional until the course and its references have been tested.

## 2. Teach the different creative decisions separately

A character's identity, visual appearance, cinematic treatment, content format, and business model are different decisions. This distinction should be taught before students collect prompts.

| Decision | Examples | Question the student answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | An authorized twin of yourself; a fictional avatar; a recurring brand character | Who is appearing? |
| Visual appearance | Photorealistic; stylized realistic; 3D cartoon; 2D illustrated/anime-inspired | What should the character look like? |
| Visual treatment | Cinematic; casual phone-camera; editorial; studio/product | How should the image or scene be lit, framed, and presented? |
| Content format | Dance; beauty transition; UGC-style ad; short drama; narrated faceless story | What will the content do? |
| Distribution and income | Audience building; brand services; affiliate sales; paid stories; platform programs | Where will it go, and what is the intended business path? |

Cinematic does not mean a different species of avatar: both a realistic person and an animated character can be presented cinematically. UGC describes a content format and presentation convention. An AI-made UGC-style ad is synthetic creative, not evidence of a real customer experience.

Also distinguish an AI character sometimes called a “model” from the generation model or engine selected in a tool.

Teach with the same simple subject across several visual examples. Compare skin/material treatment, proportions, lighting, camera framing, and intended audience. Students select one direction for their first project.

## 3. Launch curriculum — eight complete modules

The sequence below is the core product. All eight modules, their essential resources, and a completed instructor example should be ready before launch.

| Module | Course | Required member output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Choose Your Creative Direction | A one-page direction brief |
| 1 | Understand AI Looks and Art Direction | A selected visual style and a small reference board |
| 2 | Plan with ChatGPT and Organize with Claude Code | A production brief, shot list, and organized project documents |
| 3 | Higgsfield Foundations and Credit Control | A deliberate test with documented settings and costs |
| 4 | Build Your Character or AI Twin | An approved character reference pack |
| 5 | Build Your Set | An approved environment with consistent reference views |
| 6 | Build and Manage Your Assets | A reusable asset library and continuity checklist |
| 7 | Direct and Finish Your First Scene | A finished short piece and production review |

Suggested scope: 32 short lessons, usually 5–10 minutes each where practical, plus practice. These are planning targets, not completion-time guarantees. Keep tool-installation walkthroughs as support lessons so they do not overwhelm the creative sequence.

### Module 0 — Choose Your Creative Direction

Three lessons:

1. Choose one first goal: an avatar account, a branded scene, a short story, or a faceless format.
2. Identify the audience, purpose, preferred platform, visual direction, time available, and generation budget.
3. Choose a first deliverable and place other ideas in a “Later” list.

Assignment: complete the direction brief and choose one small project to finish over the first month. Completion is based on the output, not a forced pace.

Resource: direction worksheet with fields for audience, identity type, look, format, first output, budget, tool access, and later ideas.

Completion check: the member can say exactly what they will make next and what is outside that project's scope.

### Module 1 — Understand AI Looks and Art Direction

Four lessons:

1. Compare photorealistic, stylized realistic, 3D cartoon, and 2D illustrated looks.
2. Compare cinematic, casual phone-camera, editorial, and studio treatments.
3. Select references for texture, color, lighting, composition, and wardrobe; explain each reference's role.
4. Write a simple visual direction brief and identify conflicting style instructions.

Assignment: create a small reference board and choose one appearance plus one treatment. State what should remain consistent.

Resource: visual style comparison guide; reference-role worksheet; style selection checklist.

Completion check: the student can explain the intended look without relying on a long list of vague adjectives.

### Module 2 — Plan with ChatGPT and Organize with Claude Code

Four lessons:

1. Use ChatGPT to turn the direction brief into a scene concept, short script, and list of shots.
2. Write specific instructions: context, task, references, constraints, desired output, and a targeted revision.
3. Store approved project information and keep drafts separate from material ready for generation.
4. Use Claude Code to organize a small folder and update project documents; review the actual changes.

Assignment: create a production brief, character outline, shot list, and organized project documents.

Resources: brief template; prompt framework; shot-list template; starter folder example; sample project instructions.

ChatGPT Projects can group related chats, uploaded sources, and instructions. [Official OpenAI documentation](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/projects).

Claude Code can work on files and run commands. Teach it as a workspace with reviewable actions, and distinguish it from ordinary Claude chat. Its CLAUDE.md guidance supplies context rather than enforced behavior. [Claude Code overview](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/overview), [project memory documentation](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory).

Provide the same documents as a manual route for beginners who are not ready to adopt Claude Code. Add a deeper Claude Code support lesson on setup, project permissions, backups, and resuming sessions. Direct generation connections come later.

Completion check: the student can find approved material, understand what was changed, and resume the project.

### Module 3 — Higgsfield Foundations and Credit Control

Four lessons:

1. Navigate the current interface, assets, generation settings, and usage history.
2. Choose a model for the task and understand its supported inputs and controls.
3. Inspect the displayed cost and run a small test that answers a specific question.
4. Review the result and decide whether to keep, correct, simplify, switch approach, or stop.

Assignment: record one test and the reason for the next decision.

Resources: dated model decision guide; generation log; project spending worksheet; before-generation checklist.

Log the tool and route, model, prompt version, references, duration/resolution, charge, refund if any, usability, and next step. Track total net credits per usable output rather than highlighting only the best attempt.

Higgsfield documents that costs vary with the generation settings and that eligible website Unlimited/free allowances do not extend to connected agent/CLI generations. Confirm the current account and route before teaching specific costs. [Credit guidance](https://higgsfield.ai/creator-hub/help-center/credits/how-credits-work).

Completion check: the student can find the actual cost and explain whether another attempt is justified.

### Module 4 — Build Your Character or AI Twin

Five lessons:

1. Choose an authorized real-person twin or a fictional identity and define the character's purpose.
2. Build the character guide: face, complexion, body proportions, hair, age range, wardrobe, and visual style.
3. Prepare identity references, portraits, full-body views, and useful angles without treating generated variants as automatically correct.
4. Generate the approved first image and diagnose changes to the face, body, hair, hands, or clothing.
5. Define speech and personality where relevant; separate language, accent, and voice identity.

Assignment: assemble one approved identity pack and demonstrate the character in a second setup.

Resources: character guide; angle/reference checklist; voice notes; character quality-review sheet.

Teach consistent identity as a result to inspect, not a guarantee from a “lock” phrase. Use your own or authorized face/voice references. Explain the difference between rights to use an asset and rights to distribute the reference or prompt to other people.

Completion check: the member can identify unwanted changes and explain which references or decisions need revisiting.

### Module 5 — Build Your Set

Four lessons:

1. Define the location's purpose: a vanity for beauty, a kitchen or tabletop for products, a podcast corner, or a room for a story.
2. Plan the layout, furniture, background objects, color palette, lighting direction, and space for the action.
3. Establish camera height, distance, framing, and screen direction; create an empty-set reference before combining it with the character when useful.
4. Build and inspect a small set of views: establishing/wide, medium, and a relevant alternate angle.

Assignment: produce one reusable set with a written description and approved reference views.

Resources: set-building worksheet; simple layout guide; lighting and framing checklist; environment continuity sheet.

Check object placement, scale, shadows, architecture, and light direction across views. Generating a reverse angle does not guarantee that the room's geometry remains consistent. Show how to identify and correct those errors.

Completion check: the student can place their character in the environment plausibly and explain what must remain consistent in the next shot.

### Module 6 — Build and Manage Your Assets

Four lessons:

1. List assets before generating: character views, wardrobe, props, products, backgrounds, stills, voices, and motion references.
2. Prepare references for recurring objects, clothing, and hero props. For real products, preserve accurate shape, packaging, labels, and scale.
3. Give approved assets clear names and versions; label each item's purpose and source/permission.
4. Make a continuity checklist and reuse the approved material in a second scene.

Assignment: organize the identity pack, set references, and a few relevant wardrobe/prop references into one reusable library. Asset count depends on the chosen project.

Resources: asset manifest; file-naming guide; reference-rights field; approved/draft/replaced status labels; continuity checklist.

Suggested asset record fields: name or ID, category, version, reference file, description, source/permission, intended role, approved date, linked shots, and known limitations.

Completion check: the student can locate the current version of each required asset and explain what it controls in the scene.

### Module 7 — Direct and Finish Your First Scene

Four lessons:

1. Combine the character, set, assets, and shot plan into an approved still.
2. Animate one manageable action using controls available in the selected model; introduce simple dialogue only where appropriate.
3. Review continuity, motion, speech, audio, and framing; document a targeted correction.
4. Assemble and export a short piece, prepare its caption, and review total effort and credits.

Assignment: finish a small scene, typically 15–30 seconds assembled from manageable shots, with a shorter alternative when budget or tool access calls for it.

Resources: scene direction worksheet; shot review rubric; export checklist; production retrospective.

Demonstrate the actual assembly method you use. If a separate editor is needed, disclose it before the lesson and show a basic route. Public posting is optional.

Completion check: the result matches the original brief and the student can explain how to repeat the process for a new idea.

| Creative brief and prompt framework | Explain the intended result clearly | 2 |

## 5. Member pathways after the foundation

| Pathway | Suggested course order | First substantial result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Avatar and lifestyle creator | Dance/motion, then transitions, then recurring content planning | A small collection featuring one recognizable character |
| Brand content creator | UGC production, then TikTok Shop/affiliate lessons if appropriate | A product-focused portfolio with accurate representations |
| Story creator | Short stories, then paid story distribution | A coherent pilot and a defined viewing experience |

Recommend one active pathway for the first 30 days after the foundation. Members may switch based on what they learn; this is guidance rather than an artificial access restriction.

## 6. Post-launch course release map

Months are relative to launch. This is an internal planning sequence; announce firm release dates only when production and testing justify them.

| Release | Specialist course | Foundation prerequisites | Member deliverable |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Month 1 | Dance Trends and Motion Direction | Approved character, full-body reference, set, and motion basics | A short dance or motion clip with documented references |
| Month 2 | Beauty, Makeup, and Outfit Transitions | Character consistency, approved before/after states, matching framing | One clean transformation sequence |
| Month 3 | AI UGC and Product Content | Character, set, accurate product references, shot planning | A short UGC-style ad and two hook variations |
| Month 4 | Build a Faceless Content Format | Direction, scripting, asset management, finishing | Three original posts within one chosen format |
| Month 5 | AI Short Stories and Recurring Series | Shot planning, sets, multiple assets, dialogue basics | A pilot episode and a three-episode outline |
| Month 6 | Paid Story Libraries and YouTube Distribution | Completed pilot and audience/offer plan | A tested viewing and access flow |
| Month 7 | TikTok Shop and Affiliate Content | UGC production, product accuracy, audience fit | A compliant draft campaign and account eligibility checklist |

Every monthly release should include a complete small project, a reference pack, a troubleshooting session, and an update note. Members do not need to wait until all seven are released to make useful work.

### Month 1 — Dance Trends and Motion Direction

Teach how to choose a movement that fits the avatar, prepare a useful full-body reference, obtain or record an authorized motion video, match framing and starting pose, and evaluate hands, feet, floor contact, clothing, and background changes. Include audio synchronization and checking whether the soundtrack is permitted for the intended use.

Separate the stable motion lesson from dated trend examples. A new dance example can replace an old one without rebuilding the course. Do not promise exact choreography replication or viral reach.

Higgsfield describes a motion-control workflow combining a character image with a motion-reference video. Verify the currently available model and controls during course production; the linked guide is useful for the method rather than a permanent plan or pricing promise. [Higgsfield motion-control guide](https://higgsfield.ai/blog/Kling-2.6-Motion-Control-Full-Guide).

Reference pack: motion brief, reference checklist, body-framing guide, timing notes, and failure examples.

### Month 2 — Beauty, Makeup, and Outfit Transitions

Teach matched before/after assets, stable facial identity, camera angle and position, lighting continuity, transition gestures, cuts on movement, and timing. Demonstrate both an edited transition between approved shots and a generative transition where the selected tool supports it.

Cover what must change and what must stay consistent. Inspect makeup, hair, clothes, jewelry, background, and product appearance. Present synthetic beauty transformations as creative effects rather than proof that a product caused a result.

Reference pack: before/after planning sheet, transition storyboard, continuity checklist, and edit timing example.

### Month 3 — AI UGC and Product Content

Teach audience and product research, a short brief, hooks, accurate product information, the on-camera avatar or presenter, set choice, product close-ups, dialogue, and a clear call to action.

Build one room or tabletop set and reuse it in several shot types. Check packaging, labels, scale, hand-object contact, and what the real product can do. Include a client-ready delivery exercise and revision boundaries.

Reference pack: UGC brief, product fact sheet, set plan, shot list, script framework, review checklist, and sample delivery pack.

Do not present a fictional avatar as a real customer recounting an actual experience. Teach disclosure and product accuracy in the relevant platform context.

### Month 4 — Build a Faceless Content Format

Teach selection of one audience and original format, research and fact checking where needed, scripting, narration, visuals, editing, and a manageable publishing system. Examples can include fictional stories, explainers, or themed visual narratives.

Create three posts with distinct substance to test the format. Explain how to assess audience response and improve the next piece. Separate the ability to publish from eligibility for advertising or other income programs.

Reference pack: format brief, three-post content map, originality checklist, source log, and performance review sheet.

YouTube's monetization policy requires original, authentic value and addresses repetitive, mass-produced, and reused content. Faceless or AI-made content is not itself an income guarantee. [YouTube monetization policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en-GB).

### Month 5 — AI Short Stories and Recurring Series

Teach a premise, character motivations, scene structure, emotional progression, dialogue coverage, episode continuity, and a manageable production plan.

Reuse characters and sets across scenes while making each episode meaningfully different. Use your own series as a documented example where it is ready to teach, including costs and unsuccessful attempts.

Reference pack: series guide, episode outline, scene breakdown, character/voice records, asset reuse map, and production log.

### Month 6 — Paid Story Libraries and YouTube Distribution

Teach the viewer journey from a free sample or trailer to a clear paid offer, sign-in, purchase, entitled access, cancellation, and access expiry. Compare YouTube channel memberships with an independently paid site using suitably controlled video hosting.

YouTube supports members-only viewing through channel memberships for eligible creators. Check eligibility when producing this lesson. [YouTube membership requirements](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7636690?hl=en), [membership benefits](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14917424?hl=en-WS).

An unlisted YouTube embed is shareable by anyone who obtains the link. A paid page around it does not make the video itself securely restricted to paying viewers. Demonstrate this limitation and distinguish page access from video access. [YouTube privacy settings](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/157177?co=GENIE.Platform%3DDesktop&hl=en).

Assignment: map and test a small access flow with sample content; test both a paying and a non-paying viewer, and revoked/expired access. Do not require students to charge real customers to complete the lesson.

Reference pack: offer worksheet, viewing journey, platform comparison, access test checklist, and expense tracker.

The tool or creator called “Bendable” in the discussion has not been verified. Keep this example pending a link or screenshot; do not silently substitute another product or teach an assumed feature.

### Month 7 — TikTok Shop and Affiliate Content

Teach the difference between earning affiliate commission, selling a product, providing creative services, and receiving platform creator payments. Verify current country and account eligibility, product requirements, disclosure, and content rules before students build a campaign.

Use real product facts and approved reference material. Teach testing different hooks, tracking clicks/conversions where available, understanding commission and returns, and accounting for production costs. Avoid income promises.

The U.S. TikTok Shop AI policy dated September 2, 2026 permits compliant AI use, requires disclosure of fully or significantly AI-generated content, and prohibits misleading product representations or unsupported effects. Product visuals must match the actual item. Recheck this policy for the student's market and publication date. [TikTok Shop AI requirements](https://seller-us.tiktok.com/university/essay?knowledge_id=491489038501663).

Reference pack: eligibility checklist with official links and checked date; product facts worksheet; claims/disclosure review; campaign plan; commission and cost worksheet.

## 7. AI tool education

Keep ChatGPT, Claude Code, and Higgsfield as the demonstrated starting stack. Introduce an additional tool when it solves a defined problem in a course.

| Task | Starting teaching approach |
| --- | --- |
| Ideas, scripts, and instructions | Demonstrate ChatGPT |
| Project files and repeatable preparation | Demonstrate Claude Code; provide manual documents |
| Character, set, image, and video generation | Demonstrate the relevant Higgsfield route |
| Voice and dialogue | Demonstrate the supported controls in the selected route |
| Assembly, captions, and final export | Teach the editor actually used in the project; disclose any separate access |
| Paid access, checkout, and video hosting | Evaluate during the paid-stories course before recommending a stack |
| Measurement and sales | Use relevant platform analytics and an expense log |

Maintain a dated tool directory organized by task. Each entry should state who it helps, what was personally tested, costs and account requirements, compatibility, input/output examples, limitations, official documentation, and whether an affiliate relationship exists.

Label tools Tested, Under Evaluation, or Superseded. A product announcement alone does not justify a recommendation.

Add the deeper Claude Code/Higgsfield connection workshop after students understand credit spending and manual review. Higgsfield currently documents a CLI route and says conversational credit limits are not hard caps. [Higgsfield integration guidance](https://higgsfield.ai/creator-hub/help-center/integrations/how-do-i-connect-higgsfield-to-ai-agent).

## 8. Ongoing monthly update plan

Use a predictable monthly package alongside the specialist release.

| Week | Content | Member benefit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Week 1 | New specialist module and the month's project brief | A clear next project |
| Week 2 | Worked example with prompts, references, failures, and observed cost | See how the process actually behaved |
| Week 3 | Group troubleshooting and submission review | Apply the method to their own work |
| Week 4 | “What Changed in AI” digest and lesson corrections | Know what matters and which instructions changed |

The digest should answer: what changed, who it affects, what you tested, whether members need to act, which lesson was updated, and the date of verification. Prioritize relevant changes; include a short optional discovery section for interesting tools beyond the current tracks.

Maintain a visible course update log. Mark stale instructions and temporarily withdraw a workflow if it cannot be reproduced. Keep stable creative principles separate from interface recordings to reduce unnecessary rerecording.

This is a publishing plan for the membership, not a scheduled automation.

## 9. Launch production checklist

Before accepting members for the complete foundation:

- All eight foundation modules are recorded and accessible.
- Essential reference worksheets and completed examples are available.
- One complete sample connects direction, style, character, set, assets, and finished scene.
- The chosen tools, access requirements, and separate costs are clear.
- A beginner has followed the main path and confusing steps have been revised.
- Every tool demonstration has a last-tested date, model/route information, and relevant settings.
- The resource library distinguishes approved examples from experimental material.
- The support process and first monthly session are defined.
- The post-launch map separates available lessons from planned courses.

Start with one strong instructor project that can later support a transition, a UGC piece, and a short story. Reusing an established character and set makes those connections visible and reduces avoidable production work.

## 10. Teaching and reference standards

Every lesson should show the intended result, prerequisites, steps, one likely failure, an assignment, and a completion checklist. Include permission, attribution, and platform requirements where they directly affect the project.

Reference packs should record the source and permitted use of any third-party material. Keep account credentials and private information out of shared examples.

Ask students for the intended result, tool/model/route, references, prompt, settings, observed result, and what they already tried when requesting feedback.

Track completed outputs, credits per usable result, repeat questions, instructor support time, pathway selection, and renewals. Use those findings to refine later releases. Broad topic coverage is a long-term roadmap; clear progress is the immediate member experience.
